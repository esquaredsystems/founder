--- title: "Why your termination should not come as a surprise" date: 2022-05-30 permalink: "/writing/2022/may/termination-surprise/" category: "misc" description: "Two hires, two endings, and the one difference that mattered - whether anybody was surprised." ---

These days my LinkedIn feed is flooded with posts regarding recent layouts from one of the leading product based IT firms. I wholeheartedly wish the best for the employees who lost their jobs, and pray that Allah gives them strength to go through the hardship they’re likely to face. It’s definitely a worrisome position to be in, but on the brighter end there are plenty of new opportunities to seek. Having said that, I wanted to share my two cents on a completely different aspect which may annoy you but I urge you to think through before waging a war on my wall. I’ll start with two real stories.

Story 1: In my past job, we were met with a tough situation where a critical deadline was to be met and we were a developer short. We started collecting applications and conducted interviews/tests, etc. but could not see the “right” candidate (focus on the term right). We had an internal discussion and concluded that we’ll never be able to find a “perfect” candidate (note the difference) so let’s bite the bullet and go with the first one which makes sense. We ended up hiring the next applicant and I became her trainer. On her joining, she expressed that she was surprised to have been hired. Cutting short, it was a disaster. The work didn’t get done, this person was an extra weight, clients were unhappy, the team was unhappy, the bosses were unhappy. The most challenging situation was her performance review meeting where she went ballistic; she was surprised to hear that she wasn’t doing well. She was assigned another leader with results not any different. The adventure finally ended up in her resignation.

Story 2: Learning from our mistakes, we developed a proper evaluation process from written test to practical to interview. The next batch we hired was for a long term project. One of them in particular was under my direct supervision. He was struggling from day 1, he kept trying his best and I did my best to help out but writing code wasn’t his gig. He didn’t meet any objectives during probation, so we extended his period. Nothing changed. The extension also expired and his departure was decided. The termination meeting between us was one in a million, we were both relaxed and friendly. He knew he was going to get fired, but he was not surprised, he knew we both tried our best, best of all he knew exactly what the problem is and the decisions he needed to take. He did his farewell, not only to us but also software development, and changed his area. He’s now a successful professional who many won’t believe was once struggling so hard.

Now, if you paid attention, the key difference between both the cases was “surprise”. The difference between both cases was that one was reading the situation and the other was not. Let’s get back to our original matter. You were hired by a newcomer with plenty of seed money to burn and immense pressure to show progress and growth by the investors. The first thing any company trapped in this situation would do is hire the top brains on insanely high salary and perks, outbidding everyone else in the market. It’s not that they don’t know that the right way is to grow from grass root, it’s just that their clock is ticking. And now, you as an employee, are convinced that the old companies you were in have been exploiting you. The same guys who taught you how to convert a barely working piece of code into a work of art, appear as evil monsters who took you for cheap labour. Those companies remain as they were and you’re now living a dream.

Years pass, and the time for natural course correction arrives. The utopia you inhabited is now bidding farewell to its spring. The ground reality hits and hits hard, and you along with many others are no longer affordable. You were laid off. But did that outcome surprise you? Why? Were you really completely unaware of the world around you? Did you have no friends and classmates to tell you how well paid you are as compared to them? Had you never gone through the salary survey data? Were you aware of how your company was doing in the market? If none of that happened, then you have the right to be surprised, but make no mistake, in some ways you are also equally responsible for your situation.

Don’t be surprised. You can’t afford to be surprised in this game. The IT industry is not in danger, it isn’t seeing its downfall and the data is very clear on this. I won’t share any stats to save length but you’re a search query away to find out the numbers. There’s a shortage of over 1 million Software Engineers. The shutdowns, downsizings, pay cuts are signs of maturity and elimination of outliers. This isn’t how a particular company works, or a particular industry works. It’s how the world works as a whole. If you happen to still be in the utopia, and think it’s your hard work and skills alone which enabled you to keep hanging, don’t forget the clock. Now I’m not at all suggesting that you should jump out of a sailing ship, instead I’ll ask you to seek wisdom from the holy Quran:

If you’re unaware of the context, please read an explanation or ask a credible scholar. The moral of this story is to be wise and plan ahead. I have no doubt that the future will present you another opportunity, and most likely a better one. Don’t waste that opportunity. Invest in an alternate business before planning a trip to Thailand. Buy health insurance before the new iPhone. Own a piece of land before buying a car. And if there’s only one thing you can invest in, it’s your own skills.
